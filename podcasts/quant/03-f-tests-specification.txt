TITLE: Nested F-Tests, Prediction and Model Misspecification (R1)

AVA: Episode three: joint F-tests, predicting with a model, and getting the specification right.
[PAUSE 1]
AVA: What is a nested model?
ANDREW: The unrestricted model has all the variables. The restricted model drops some, so it's nested inside the full one. We test whether the dropped slopes are all zero. If we drop b two and b three, H-nought says b two equals b three equals zero.
AVA: The formula?
ANDREW: F equals, SSE restricted minus SSE unrestricted, divided by q, all over SSE unrestricted divided by n minus k minus 1. q is the number of variables dropped, and k is the number of variables in the full model. Reject if F is above the critical value.
AVA: Example.
ANDREW: The rent model. Unrestricted: three variables, SSE 13,550.64. Restricted: one variable, SSE 32,627.29. n is 191, so q is 2. The numerator is 32,627.29 minus 13,550.64, divided by 2, which is 9,538.3. The denominator is 13,550.64 divided by 187, which is 72.46. F equals 131.63. The critical F with 2 and 187 degrees of freedom is 3.04. Reject: at least one of the dropped variables matters.
AVA: Why not just run two t-tests?
ANDREW: Because the Xs are usually correlated. The joint test measures their combined explanatory power properly.
[PAUSE 1]
AVA: And the F-test for the whole model?
ANDREW: It's the same test with every slope dropped. The restricted model is just Y equals b zero, so all the variation is unexplained, and SSE restricted equals SST. q equals k. The formula collapses to RSS over k, divided by SSE over n minus k minus 1. That's MSR over MSE.
AVA: Numbers.
ANDREW: Four variables, 65 observations, SST 9,105, SSE 4,320. RSS is 9,105 minus 4,320, which is 4,785. MSR is 4,785 over 4, which is 1,196.25. MSE is 4,320 over 60, which is 72. F equals 16.61.
AVA: And dropping just the last two, with the restricted SSE at 5,105?
ANDREW: 5,105 minus 4,320 is 785. Divide by 2 to get 392.5, then divide by 72. F equals 5.45. The trap answer is 392: that's the numerator before you divide by MSE.
[PAUSE 1]
AVA: How do you predict Y?
ANDREW: Use every coefficient, including the insignificant ones. Earnings growth equals minus 11.6 plus 0.25 times payout plus 0.14 times yield curve slope. Payout 50 percent and slope 4 percent: minus 11.6, plus 12.5, plus 0.56, gives 1.46 percent. If you want to drop a variable, re-estimate the model. Don't just delete its term.
[PAUSE 1]
AVA: Now specification. What makes a good model?
ANDREW: Every variable has an economic reason to be there. The model is parsimonious, the functional form is right, it works out of sample, and it violates no key assumption.
AVA: And the four types of misspecification?
ANDREW: One: omitting a variable. If the omitted variable is correlated with the ones left in, every coefficient becomes biased and inconsistent. If it's uncorrelated, only the intercept is biased. Two: a variable that should be transformed. Market cap, for example, works in logs, not levels. Three: inappropriate scaling, like using the number of free-float shares instead of free float as a proportion. Four: incorrectly pooling data, like running one regression across before and after the pandemic when you should run two.
AVA: What does misspecification do?
ANDREW: It gives biased and inconsistent coefficients, unreliable hypothesis tests and poor forecasts. Unbiased coefficients are not one of the results. That's the usual "least likely" answer.
AVA: Three properties of a good estimator?
ANDREW: Unbiased: right on average. Efficient: the smallest variance among the unbiased estimators. Consistent: more accurate as the sample grows.
[PAUSE 1]
AVA: Memory hook.
ANDREW: Nested F: extra error from dropping q variables, per variable, over MSE. Overall F: drop them all, so it's MSR over MSE. Predict with every coefficient. Omit a correlated variable and every coefficient is biased. Pool two regimes and you have mixed two models.
