TITLE: Equivalencies, Caps and Floors (R29)

AVA: Episode eight. Equivalencies, which is how derivatives combine.
ANDREW: One question unlocks them all: who gains when rates rise? The long rate call, the long FRA and the payer swap gain. The long rate put, the short FRA and the receiver swap gain when rates fall.
[PAUSE 1]
AVA: The building block.
ANDREW: A long rate call plus a short rate put equals a long FRA. A short call plus a long put equals a short FRA. Both need the same strike, equal to the FRA rate.
[PAUSE 1]
AVA: Caps and floors.
ANDREW: A cap is a series of rate calls, called caplets, with the same strike and different maturities. It protects a floating-rate borrower. With a 5 percent cap, if rates go to 7 the borrower pays 7, gets 2 back from the cap, and nets 5. If rates fall to 2, they pay just 2. They're never above 5, and they keep the benefit of falling rates.
ANDREW: A floor is a series of rate puts, called floorlets. It protects a floating-rate lender or bondholder. With a 5 percent floor, if rates fall to 2 the floor pays 3, so the holder still earns 5.
[PAUSE 1]
AVA: Building bigger.
ANDREW: A long cap plus a short floor equals a payer swap. A short cap plus a long floor equals a receiver swap. A swap is worth zero at the start, so if the strike equals the swap rate, the cap and the floor are worth the same.
ANDREW: With swaptions: a long receiver swaption plus a short payer swaption equals a forward receiver swap. Flip both and you get a forward payer swap.
ANDREW: And a callable bond equals an option-free bond plus a short receiver swaption, because the issuer calls the bond when rates fall.
[PAUSE 1]
AVA: Memory hook.
ANDREW: Call plus short put equals FRA. Calls build a cap, for borrowers. Puts build a floor, for lenders. Cap minus floor equals payer swap. Callable bond equals bond minus receiver swaption.
